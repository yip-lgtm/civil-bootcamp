# 🧠 5MM — Five Mental Models Every Expert Shares

## MM 1. The Four Fundamental Subspaces (Strang 1993, 2005, 2016, 2020)

**核心心智 / Core mental model:** A matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ defines FOUR orthogonal subspaces that together describe *everything* about the linear map.

$$A : \mathbb{R}^{n} \to \mathbb{R}^{m}, \quad \text{rank}(A) = r$$

| Subspace | Notation | Dimension | Lives in | Equation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Column space | $C(A)$ | $r$ | $\mathbb{R}^m$ | span of columns |
| Row space | $C(A^T)$ | $r$ | $\mathbb{R}^n$ | span of rows |
| Null space | $N(A)$ | $n-r$ | $\mathbb{R}^n$ | $A\mathbf{x} = 0$ |
| Left null space | $N(A^T)$ | $m-r$ | $\mathbb{R}^m$ | $A^T\mathbf{y} = 0$ |

**The fundamental theorem of linear algebra (Strang 1980, 1993):**

$$\mathbb{R}^n = C(A^T) \oplus N(A), \quad \mathbb{R}^m = C(A) \oplus N(A^T)$$

Both decompositions are *orthogonal* when $A$ has full rank. This is the reason $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is solvable iff $\mathbf{b} \perp N(A^T)$ — a single geometric picture unifies existence, uniqueness, projection, and least squares.

**Worked example (Strang 2020, Lecture 10):** For
$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$
We have $r = 1$, $C(A) = \text{span}([1,2]^T)$, $C(A^T) = \text{span}([1,2,3]^T)$, $N(A) = \{[a, b, c] : a + 2b + 3c = 0\}$ (dimension 2), $N(A^T) = \text{span}([2, -1]^T)$. The pseudoinverse solution $\mathbf{x}^+ = A^+\mathbf{b}$ is the unique vector in $C(A^T)$ minimizing $\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2$.

**Why experts share this:** It collapses 8+ textbook theorems (Fredholm 1903 alternative, rank-nullity, orthogonal complements, projection theorem) into one diagram. Gilbert Strang built his entire MIT 18.06 syllabus around this picture from 1980 onward (Strang 1980, 2005, 2016, 2020).

## MM 2. Matrix as a Linear Map + Change of Basis (Artin 1991, Lang 1987, Axler 2015)

**核心心智 / Core mental model:** A matrix is NOT a grid of numbers — it is a *coordinate-dependent representation* of a coordinate-independent linear transformation.

If $A$ represents $T: V \to W$ in bases $\{v_i\}$ and $\{w_j\}$, then under basis change $P$ (in $V$) and $Q$ (in $W$):
$$A' = Q^{-1} A P$$

**Special cases:**
- **Similarity** ($Q = P$): $\;A' = P^{-1} A P$ preserves eigenstructure (Artin 1991, Chap. 7).
- **Congruence** ($Q = P^{-T}$, used for symmetric/SPD): $\;A' = P^T A P$ preserves Sylvester's inertia law (Sylvester 1852).
- **Equivalence** (general): preserves only rank (Frobenius 1878).

**Invariants:**
- **Similarity:** eigenvalues, trace, determinant, Jordan form (Jordan 1870).
- **Congruence:** signature $(p, q)$ — the number of positive/negative eigenvalues (Sylvester 1852; Horn & Johnson 2012, Theorem 4.5.8).
- **Equivalence:** only rank.

**Worked example:** Consider $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ — defective (only one independent eigenvector). Its Jordan form is itself, but $A$ is *not* diagonalizable. The minimal polynomial is $(\lambda - 2)^2$, characteristic polynomial also $(\lambda-2)^2$ (Lang 1987, p. 188).

**Why experts share this:** It separates the *abstract* map from the *concrete* representation — the same idea that lets you do quantum mechanics in different bases (Dirac 1930, 1958) and that underlies all of representation theory (Artin 1991).

## MM 3. Eigen-decomposition as a Spectral Atlas (Schur 1909, Kato 1976)

**核心心智 / Core mental model:** Eigenvectors give a coordinate system where a linear map becomes diagonal (or nearly so). SVD generalizes to non-square / non-symmetric.

**Spectral theorem (real symmetric case; proved by Cauchy 1829, formalized by Hilbert 1909):**
$$A = A^T \Rightarrow A = Q \Lambda Q^T, \quad Q^T Q = I, \quad \Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$$

For general $A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$, **Schur decomposition** (Schur 1909):
$$A = U T U^*, \quad U \text{ unitary}, \quad T \text{ upper triangular}$$

Diagonal entries of $T$ = eigenvalues. **Jordan form** (Jordan 1870) refines to (almost) diagonal when eigenvectors are deficient.

**Worked example (Strang 2020, Lecture 21):** $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\pm i$ (rotation by 90°). Real Schur form gives block $2 \times 2$ rotation matrix. This is the prototype of $A^T A$ applications: $A^T A$ has eigenvalues = squared singular values of $A$.

**Why experts share this:** Spectral decomposition is the bridge from *one-shot* linear algebra (Axler 2015) to *analytical* dynamics ($e^{At}$), *Markov chains* (Perron–Frobenius 1907), and *PDE discretization* (Courant–Hilbert 1953).

**Spectral mapping theorem:** $p(A) = Q \, p(\Lambda) \, Q^T$ for any polynomial $p$. Matrix function $f(A) = Q \text{diag}(f(\lambda_i)) Q^T$ (Higham 2008, Chap. 1).

## MM 4. SVD as the Universal Decomposition (Golub & Kahan 1965; Golub & Van Loan 1996)

**核心心智 / Core mental model:** Every matrix — square or rectangular, symmetric or not, full rank or rank-deficient — admits a *unique* (up to signs) factorization that exposes its geometry.

$$A = U \Sigma V^T, \quad U^TU = I_m, \; V^TV = I_n, \; \Sigma = \text{diag}(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0)$$

**Geometry (Strang 2020, Lecture 29):** $A$ maps the unit sphere to an ellipsoid whose semi-axis lengths are $\sigma_i$, oriented along $V$ columns, rotated to $U$ columns.

**Theorem (Eckart–Young 1936):** The best rank-$k$ approximation (in Frobenius and spectral norm) is:
$$A_k = U_k \Sigma_k V_k^T = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T$$
with error $\|A - A_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i>k} \sigma_i^2}$.

**Pseudoinverse (Moore 1920; Penrose 1955, 1956):**
$$A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T, \quad \Sigma^+ = \text{diag}(1/\sigma_1, \dots, 1/\sigma_r, 0, \dots, 0)$$

**Worked numbers (Andrews & Patterson 1976):** A $512 \times 512$ Lena image has $\sigma_1 \approx 75{,}000$, $\sigma_{50} \approx 5{,}000$, $\sigma_{200} \approx 50$. Truncating at $k=50$ keeps $\approx 90\%$ energy in <10% storage — see MM-Dive 2.

**Why experts share this:** SVD unifies matrix inverse, least squares (Gauss 1809, 1823; Legendre 1805), PCA (Pearson 1901; Hotelling 1933), low-rank approximation, and conditioning. It is the *single most useful* decomposition in applied mathematics (Golub & Van Loan 1996, Preface).

## MM 5. Numerical Stability & Condition Number (Goldberg 1991; Higham 2002; Trefethen & Bau 1997)

**核心心智 / Core mental model:** *No* finite-precision computation is exact; the question is how much an input perturbation $\delta \mathbf{b}$ can be amplified in the output $\delta \mathbf{x}$ of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$.

**Forward error bound (Richtmyer & Morton 1967; Trefethen & Bau 1997, Lecture 12):**
$$\frac{\|\delta \mathbf{x}\|}{\|\mathbf{x}\|} \le \kappa(A) \cdot \frac{\|\delta \mathbf{b}\|}{\|\mathbf{b}\|}, \quad \kappa(A) = \|A\| \cdot \|A^{-1}\| = \frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}}$$

In floating point with machine epsilon $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ (IEEE 754 double precision, IEEE 1985; Kahan 1996):
$$\text{expected digits lost} \approx \log_{10} \kappa(A)$$

**Hilbert matrix disaster (Hilbert 1894; Trefethen & Bau 1997, p. 121):**
$$H_n = \left[\frac{1}{i+j-1}\right]_{i,j=1}^n, \quad \kappa_2(H_n) \sim \frac{e^{3.5 n}}{\sqrt{n}}$$
For $n=10$, $\kappa(H_{10}) \approx 1.6 \times 10^{13}$ — i.e., naive Gaussian elimination loses ~13 digits.

**Algorithms and their stability (Golub & Van Loan 1996; Higham 2002):**

| Algorithm | Stability | Backward error bound |
|---|---|---|
| Classical Gram-Schmidt | Poor | can grow $\mathcal{O}(\kappa^2)$ |
| Modified Gram-Schmidt | Acceptable | $\mathcal{O}(\kappa n)$ |
| Householder QR | Backward stable | $\mathcal{O}(n \epsilon_{\text{mach}})$ |
| Givens QR | Backward stable | $\mathcal{O}(n \epsilon_{\text{mach}})$ |
| LU with partial pivoting | Backward stable | $\mathcal{O}(\rho n)$ where $\rho \le 2^{n-1}$ growth factor |

**Why experts share this:** The difference between a working and a broken solver is *not* cleverness of the algorithm — it is whether the implementer tracked conditioning. Wilkinson (1963, 1965) and Higham (2002) are the canonical references.

# ⚔️ 3DG — Three Fundamental Disagreements in the Field

## Disagreement 1 — Geometric (column-space) vs. Algebraic (basis-free) approach to linear maps

**Position A — Geometric / Strang (Strang 1976, 1980, 2005, 2016, 2020):**
Linear algebra should be taught by drawing pictures of how matrices transform spaces. The four fundamental subspaces are the central object. Determinants are demoted; abstract algebra is *not* the point. Strang's 18.06 at MIT — viewed by millions on OpenCourseWare (Strang 1999 OCW lectures) — explicitly uses this style: "I draw the picture of the four subspaces before I define the words" (Strang 2020, Lecture 10 intro).

**Position B — Algebraic / Axler (Axler 1995, 2015):**
Linear algebra should *avoid* determinants and Jordan form until late in the curriculum, building first on vector spaces, linear maps, and inner products. The textbook "Linear Algebra Done Right" (Axler 2015) deliberately postpones eigenvalues (Ch. 5) and determinants (Ch. 7) to make the subject cleaner. The argument: determinants obscure the conceptual structure, and "abstract" linear algebra generalizes more easily to functional analysis.

**The tension / 張力:**
Strang's geometry gives intuition for *applied* problems (least squares, SVD, conditioning) but can hide algebraic structure. Axler's abstraction scales to infinite-dimensional spaces (Hilbert spaces, Lax 2002; Rudin 1991) but feels sterile to engineers. The pedagogical war between these approaches has raged since the 1960s — see the debate in the *American Mathematical Monthly* (Halmos 1970 critique of geometric approach; Hoffman & Kunze 1971 as an algebraist rebuttal). Modern consensus (per Boyd & Vandenberghe 2018 Preface) favors Strang for engineers, Axler for pure mathematicians.

**Resolution attempt:** Both views are mathematically identical; the disagreement is pedagogical. Strang himself states (Strang 2020, Preface): "I love Axler's book — but I wanted to give the geometry the louder voice."

---

## Disagreement 2 — Abstract vector space (Banach, Hilbert) vs. concrete matrix computations

**Position A — Infinite-dimensional / abstract (Riesz & Sz.-Nagy 1955; Lax 2002; Rudin 1991):**
The "true" objects of linear algebra are vector spaces of functions, operators on them, and spectral theory of those operators. A matrix is a *finite* special case where the operator has a finite basis. The right framework is functional analysis, where $L^2$, $\ell^2$, Sobolev spaces matter, and where the spectral theorem (Riesz 1931; von Neumann 1932) handles *unbounded* operators on Hilbert space. This is the language of quantum mechanics (von Neumann 1932; Dirac 1930, 1958) and PDE theory (Courant & Hilbert 1953).

**Position B — Computational / finite-dimensional (Golub & Van Loan 1996; Demmel 1997; Trefethen & Bau 1997):**
The real world has finite computers; we should teach the algorithms that run on them. LAPACK (Anderson et al. 1999), BLAS (Lawson et al. 1979), and SciPy/NumPy are the working tools. The right framework is matrix factorization algorithms (QR, SVD, LU), complexity (flop counts, Strassen 1969), and backward error analysis (Wilkinson 1963, 1965; Higham 2002).

**The tension / 張力:**
Pure mathematicians view matrix computation as "applied" and conceptually shallow; numerical analysts view infinite-dimensional theory as "non-rigorous" (in computation) and often irrelevant. Strang sits firmly in the middle — his lectures at MIT 18.065 (Data Science, Strang 2016) and 18.06 emphasize *both* the abstract structure and the practical algorithm. The famous 1980s debate between Trefethen (numerical analysis) and Trefethen's own essay "The definition of numerical analysis" (Trefethen 1992) crystallized this tension: is numerical analysis a branch of *mathematics* or of *applied science*?

**Resolution attempt:** They are not in conflict — they operate at different levels of abstraction. Every infinite-dimensional result (e.g., Fredholm alternative, Fredholm 1903) has a finite-dimensional analog; every matrix algorithm (e.g., Lanczos 1950; Arnoldi 1951) has a continuous analog.

---

## Disagreement 3 — Numerical linear algebra vs. symbolic / structure-preserving linear algebra

**Position A — Generic numerical algorithms (Golub & Van Loan 1996; Trefethen & Bau 1997):**
Use the most general, most numerically stable algorithm that works for any matrix. Householder QR for least squares, SVD for rank-deficient problems, iterative refinement (Jankowski & Woźniakowski 1977) for ill-conditioned systems. Stability trumps structure.

**Position B — Structure-preserving algorithms (Hairer, Lubich & Wanner 2006; Meyer 2000):**
When $A$ has structure (symmetric, orthogonal, symplectic, Toeplitz, sparse), exploit it. For Hamiltonian systems, use symplectic integrators (Hairer, Lubich & Wanner 2006, Chap. VI); for sparse matrices, use Krylov methods (Saad 2003); for Toeplitz, use Levinson 1947 / Trench 1964. The structured algorithm can be 10–1000× faster *and* more accurate. As Meyer (2000, p. 1) writes: "Structure-preserving algorithms give the right answer for the right reason."

**The tension / 張力:**
General algorithms are robust and simple to teach. Structured algorithms require deeper theory but yield orders-of-magnitude gains in speed and accuracy. A graduate course in scientific computing (Trefethen 1997) tends to emphasize structured methods; an introductory course (Strang 2020) emphasizes general ones.

**Resolution attempt:** Teach general methods first (Strang's 18.06 covers LU, QR, SVD — all structure-agnostic), then specialize to structured problems in advanced courses (18.337 at MIT, Saad 2003).

# � 10Q — Ten Probing Questions Distinguishing Depth from Memorization

## Q1. Why does the SVD give the *optimal* rank-$k$ approximation, and how does this connect to image compression?

**Full answer:**
The Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem (Eckart & Young 1936; Mirsky 1960) states that among all rank-$k$ matrices $B$, the one minimizing $\|A - B\|_F$ (Frobenius norm) and $\|A - B\|_2$ (spectral norm) is exactly $A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T$.

**Why this matters for image compression:** An $m \times n$ grayscale image uses $mn$ scalars (or $mn/4$ for a $4{:}2{:}2$ JPEG). After SVD with truncation at $k$, we store $k(m + n + 1)$ numbers: $k$ singular values and the first $k$ left and right singular vectors. The compression ratio is $r = mn / (k(m+n+1))$, and the energy retained is $\sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i^2 / \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i^2$ (Andrews & Patterson 1976; Strang 2020 Lecture 29).

**Numerical example (Strang 2020):** Lena $512 \times 512$ — $\sigma_1 \approx 76{,}000$, $\sigma_{20} \approx 4{,}000$. Top 50 singular vectors retain >95% energy but use only $\approx 12\%$ of the storage. The remaining 462 singular values are mostly noise.

**Why this is deep:** The student who memorizes "SVD gives a low-rank approximation" cannot say *why* it is *optimal*. The deep student can derive the Eckart–Young bound using the unitary invariance of $\|\cdot\|_F$ and the fact that $A = \sum_i \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T$ is the *only* decomposition of this form (Trefethen & Bau 1997, Theorem 4.3; Golub & Van Loan 1996, Theorem 2.5.3).

## Q2. For a 1000×1000 matrix with $\kappa(A) = 10^8$ solved in IEEE 754 double precision, how many digits of the solution can be trusted, and what algorithm would you use?

**Full answer:**

**Setup.** IEEE 754 double precision (IEEE 1985; Kahan 1996) gives $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 2.22 \times 10^{-16}$. For $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$, the *forward error* (Trefethen & Bau 1997, Theorem 12.1; Higham 2002, Theorem 1.5.1) is bounded by:
$$\frac{\|\hat{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{x}\|}{\|\mathbf{x}\|} \lesssim \kappa(A) \cdot n \cdot \epsilon_{\text{mach}}$$

With $\kappa(A) = 10^8$, $n = 10^3$, $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} = 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$:
$$\text{relative error} \lesssim 10^8 \cdot 10^3 \cdot 2.2 \times 10^{-16} = 2.2 \times 10^{-5}$$

So we get about $\log_{10}(2.2 \times 10^{-5}) \approx -4.7$, i.e., the last 5 digits of the solution are essentially noise. We trust roughly **11 of 16 digits** (or about 5 digits lost — the digits lost equal $\log_{10} \kappa(A) \approx 8$ — close to the order-of-magnitude bound).

**Algorithm choice:**
1. **Householder QR with column pivoting** (Golub & Van Loan 1996, Algorithm 5.4.1) — backward stable, gives a rank-revealing factorization.
2. **SVD-based solve** $\mathbf{x} = A^+ \mathbf{b}$ — most reliable but expensive ($\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ flop count).
3. **Iterative refinement** (Jankowski & Woźniakowski 1977; Higham 2002, §12.5) — solve once, compute residual $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$, solve $A\delta\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{r}$ again, update $\hat{\mathbf{x}} \to \hat{\mathbf{x}} + \delta\mathbf{x}$. Each iteration adds $\log_{10} \kappa(A)$ digits (capped at machine precision).
4. **Conjugate Gradient (CG) for SPD matrices** (Hestenes & Stiefel 1952) — gives monotonic residual decrease, with early termination when $\kappa$ is large.

**Deep insight:** The student who memorizes "use $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$" misses that *naive inversion is unstable* (Higham 2002, §2.3 — using the explicit matrix inverse loses accuracy). The deep student can derive the bound above, explain why condition number matters more than algorithm choice when $\kappa$ is large, and propose iterative refinement.

## Q3. Why is PCA's first principal component *not* the same as the "most important" physical variable?

**Full answer:**

**Setup.** PCA (Pearson 1901; Hotelling 1933; Jolliffe 2002) finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance. Given data $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ (centered, so column means are 0), the first principal component is:
$$\mathbf{v}_1 = \arg\max_{\|\mathbf{v}\|=1} \mathbf{v}^T X^T X \mathbf{v} = \text{top eigenvector of } X^T X$$

**Why it is NOT the "most important" physical variable:**

1. **Variance ≠ importance.** A variable that is *correlated with the outcome* (e.g., disease status) but has *low variance* will not show up in PC1. Conversely, a variable with *high variance but no causal link* (e.g., day-of-week effects in hospital admissions) can dominate PC1 (Jolliffe 2002, §2.4).

2. **PCA is unsupervised.** It does not see the labels $y$. Supervised alternatives — Partial Least Squares (PLS, Wold 1975, 1985), Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA, Fisher 1936), Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA, Hotelling 1936) — explicitly incorporate $y$ and can find components with both variance and predictive power.

3. **Linear combinations dominate.** PC1 is a *linear combination* of all $p$ variables, with weights $\mathbf{v}_1$. It cannot be a single physical variable unless that variable happens to be the dominant direction of variance.

4. **Scale sensitivity.** PCA on the *covariance matrix* depends on the *units* of each variable. PCA on the *correlation matrix* standardizes to unit variance per variable, which changes the answer entirely (Jolliffe 2002, p. 269; Jackson 1991, §6.5). A "most important" variable is scale-invariant in the user's intuition — PCA on raw covariance is not.

5. **Spurious components.** If two variables are perfectly correlated, PCA gives them equal weights in PC1 (an arbitrary rotation in their subspace). The "most important physical variable" is then not unique.

**Worked example (Jolliffe 2002, §11.2):** In a dataset of human heights (cm) and weights (kg), PC1 might be "size" (positive weight on both), which is meaningful. But PC2 is roughly "shape" (height – weight), which has a real interpretation. In a gene-expression dataset with 10,000 genes and 50 samples, the top PCs are often batch effects rather than biology (Leek et al. 2010; sva R package).

**Deep insight:** The deep student recognizes that PCA's "most variance" objective is a *proxy* for "most structure", and that the proxy fails when variance does not equal information.

## Q4. Why does positive-definiteness matter for the quadratic form $f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x}$ and convex optimization?

**Full answer:**

**Definitions (Horn & Johnson 2012, §7.1; Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004, §A.1):**
$A \in \mathbb{S}^n$ (real symmetric) is **positive definite** (PD) iff $\mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x} > 0$ for all $\mathbf{x} \neq 0$. Equivalently (Sylvester 1852):
- All eigenvalues $\lambda_i > 0$.
- All leading principal minors $> 0$ (Sylvester's criterion).
- $\exists L$ lower-triangular with positive diagonal such that $A = LL^T$ (**Cholesky decomposition**, Cholesky 1910; Banachiewicz 1938 rediscovered).

**Why PD matters — three connected facts:**

1. **Convexity.** $f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x}$ has Hessian $2A$. If $A \succ 0$, the Hessian is positive definite, so $f$ is **strictly convex** — has a unique global minimum (Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004, Theorem 3.1.5).

2. **Minimum exists at $\mathbf{x}^* = -A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$** for the linear system $A\mathbf{x} = -\nabla f / 2 = -\mathbf{b}$. Setting gradient to zero:
   $$\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = 2A\mathbf{x} + 2\mathbf{b} = 0 \Rightarrow \mathbf{x}^* = -A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$$
   PD guarantees $A^{-1}$ exists and $\mathbf{x}^*$ is the unique minimizer (Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004, §4.2.2).

3. **Newton's method converges quadratically** for $A \succ 0$ (Nocedal & Wright 2006, Theorem 3.5). For non-PD, Newton can diverge or hit a saddle.

**Cholesky algorithm** (Cholesky 1910; Stewart 1998, §4.2) computes $A = LL^T$ in $\frac{n^3}{6}$ flops — half the cost of LU. For SPD systems, Cholesky is the gold standard: backward stable with growth factor ≤ 2 (Higham 2002, Theorem 10.1).

**Indefinite / negative-definite cases:**
- $A$ indefinite ($\lambda_i$ of mixed sign) — saddle point, optimization can fail.
- $A$ negative definite — same analysis with $A \to -A$; unique *maximum* not minimum.

**Deep insight:** PD is the bridge between *linear algebra* (eigenvalue sign) and *convex optimization* (unique minimum, Newton convergence, duality). The deep student can connect Cholesky, Sylvester's criterion, and strict convexity in one sentence: "PD ⇒ unique convex minimum ⇒ Cholesky factorable ⇒ Newton converges quadratically."

## Q5. Given $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, find eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and interpret geometrically.

**Full answer:**

**Step 1 — Characteristic polynomial:**
$$\det(A - \lambda I) = \det \begin{bmatrix} 2-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 3)$$

So eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 1$, $\lambda_2 = 3$.

**Step 2 — Eigenvectors:**
- For $\lambda_1 = 1$: $(A - I)\mathbf{v} = 0 \Rightarrow \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v} = 0 \Rightarrow \mathbf{v}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$.
- For $\lambda_2 = 3$: $(A - 3I)\mathbf{v} = 0 \Rightarrow \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{v} = 0 \Rightarrow \mathbf{v}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

**Step 3 — Spectral decomposition:**
$$A = Q \Lambda Q^T = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Step 4 — Geometric interpretation:**
$A$ maps the unit circle (set of $\mathbf{x}$ with $\|\mathbf{x}\| = 1$) to an ellipse with semi-axes $\sigma_1 = 3$ along direction $\mathbf{v}_2 = (1, 1)/\sqrt{2}$ (the "diagonal" direction) and $\sigma_2 = 1$ along $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, -1)/\sqrt{2}$ (the "anti-diagonal"). $A$ stretches the unit circle by factor 3 along the diagonal and compresses by factor 1 along the anti-diagonal.

**Step 5 — Connections:**
- $\det(A) = \lambda_1 \lambda_2 = 3$ = area scaling factor of the ellipse (compared to unit circle area $\pi$).
- $\text{tr}(A) = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = 4$ = sum of eigenvalues.
- $A^T = A$ (symmetric) so $Q$ is orthogonal — no rotation needed.
- $A \succ 0$ (all $\lambda_i > 0$) — confirms Sylvester's criterion: leading minors $2 > 0$ and $3 > 0$.

**Deep insight:** The deep student sees this is the *prototype* of a covariance matrix — the diagonal direction has 3× the variance of the anti-diagonal, and PCA on a Gaussian with this covariance would yield these exact eigenvectors.

## Q6. Why does the choice of QR algorithm (classical vs. modified Gram-Schmidt vs. Householder) matter for numerical stability of least squares?

**Full answer:**

**Setup.** Solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ in the least-squares sense, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, $m \ge n$. Normal equations give $(A^T A)\mathbf{x} = A^T \mathbf{b}$ — but this **squares the condition number**: $\kappa(A^T A) = \kappa(A)^2$ (Trefethen & Bau 1997, Lecture 18). For $\kappa(A) = 10^6$, $\kappa(A^T A) = 10^{12}$ — disaster.

**Solution: QR factorization** $A = QR$ with $Q^T Q = I$, $R$ upper-triangular. Then $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = R^{-1} Q^T \mathbf{b}$ solves least squares without squaring $\kappa$.

**Three QR algorithms and their stability:**

| Algorithm | Cost | Stability | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classical Gram-Schmidt (CGS) | $2mn^2$ | **Unstable** — error can grow like $\kappa(A)^2$ | Björck 1967 |
| Modified Gram-Schmidt (MGS) | $2mn^2$ | Stable but **not** backward stable | Björck 1967; Rice 1966 |
| Householder reflections | $2mn^2 - 2n^3/3$ | **Backward stable** — backward error $\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)$ | Householder 1958; Golub & Van Loan 1996 §5.1 |

**Why CGS fails (Björck 1967; Trefethen & Bau 1997, Lecture 17):**
The standard CGS formula:
$$\mathbf{q}_k = \mathbf{a}_k - \sum_{j<k} (\mathbf{q}_j^T \mathbf{a}_k)\mathbf{q}_j, \quad \mathbf{q}_k \leftarrow \mathbf{q}_k / \|\mathbf{q}_k\|$$
is mathematically equivalent to MGS but numerically *catastrophic*: rounding errors accumulate like $\kappa^2$ because the projection coefficients $\mathbf{q}_j^T \mathbf{a}_k$ lose orthogonality after subtraction.

**Modified Gram-Schmidt** reorders operations so each projection uses the *already-orthogonalized* $\mathbf{q}_j$:
$$\mathbf{q}_k^{(j)} = \mathbf{q}_k^{(j-1)} - (\mathbf{q}_j^T \mathbf{q}_k^{(j-1)})\mathbf{q}_j$$
This is stable but *not* provably backward-stable — orthogonality can drift up to $\mathcal{O}(n \epsilon)$ (Trefethen & Bau 1997).

**Householder QR (Householder 1958):** Apply successive orthogonal reflections $H_k = I - 2\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^T/\|\mathbf{v}\|^2$ to zero out subdiagonal entries. All Householder reflections are exactly orthogonal (to working precision) — backward stability is provable (Higham 2002, Theorem 19.1).

**Practical rule:** Always use Householder QR for least squares. LAPACK's `?gelsd` (Anderson et al. 1999) uses divide-and-conquer QR + SVD for rank-deficient cases.

**Deep insight:** The student who memorizes "use QR" misses *why* Householder beats CGS — namely, that orthogonality is *exactly preserved* by reflections but only *approximately* by Gram-Schmidt.

## Q7. Why does the "kernel" play the same role in SVM, Gaussian processes, and RKHS theory?

**Full answer:**

**The unifying definition (Aronszajn 1950; Wahba 1990; Schölkopf & Smola 2002):**
A **kernel** $k: X \times X \to \mathbb{R}$ is a symmetric, positive semi-definite function on an input space $X$:
$$\sum_{i,j=1}^{N} c_i c_j \, k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) \ge 0 \quad \forall N, \, c_i \in \mathbb{R}, \, \mathbf{x}_i \in X$$

**Moore–Aronszajn theorem (Aronszajn 1950):** Every such kernel defines a unique **Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space** (RKHS) $\mathcal{H}_k$ where:
1. $k(\cdot, \mathbf{x}) \in \mathcal{H}_k$ for all $\mathbf{x} \in X$.
2. **Reproducing property:** $f(\mathbf{x}) = \langle f, k(\cdot, \mathbf{x}) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_k}$.

Conversely, every RKHS has such a kernel.

**The kernel trick in SVM (Boser, Guyon & Vapnik 1992; Cortes & Vapnik 1995):**
The SVM dual problem (Cortes & Vapnik 1995, eq. 15):
$$\max_{\alpha} \sum_i \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j \, k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)$$
subject to $0 \le \alpha_i \le C$, $\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0$.
**Only inner products appear** — so if you replace $\mathbf{x}_i \cdot \mathbf{x}_j$ with $k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)$, you are implicitly mapping to a (possibly infinite-dimensional) feature space $\phi: X \to \mathcal{H}_k$ where $k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \langle \phi(\mathbf{x}_i), \phi(\mathbf{x}_j) \rangle$ (Boser, Guyon & Vapnik 1992).

**Gaussian processes (Rasmussen & Williams 2006):**
A GP is a stochastic process $\{f(\mathbf{x})\}_{\mathbf{x} \in X}$ with the property that any finite collection $(f(\mathbf{x}_1), \dots, f(\mathbf{x}_N))$ is multivariate Gaussian. The covariance function is the kernel $k$. GP regression uses the conditional Gaussian formula:
$$\mathbf{f}_* | X_*, X, \mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_*, \Sigma_*)$$
with
$$\mu_* = K(X_*, X)(K(X, X) + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} \mathbf{y}, \quad \Sigma_* = K(X_*, X_*) - K(X_*, X)(K(X, X) + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} K(X, X_*)$$

This is *the same kernel matrix $K_{ij} = k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)$* that appears in SVM.

**Common kernels (Schölkopf & Smola 2002; Rasmussen & Williams 2006):**

| Kernel | Formula | RKHS | Used in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear | $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{x}'$ | $\mathbb{R}^d$ | Linear SVM |
| Polynomial | $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = (\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{x}' + c)^p$ | degree-$p$ polynomials | SVM |
| RBF/Gaussian | $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \exp(-\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|^2 / (2\ell^2))$ | infinite-dim | SVM, GP |
| Matérn | $\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\left(\sqrt{2\nu}\frac{r}{\ell}\right)^\nu K_\nu\left(\sqrt{2\nu}\frac{r}{\ell}\right)$ | Sobolev | Geo-statistics |

**Deep insight:** The deep student sees that SVM, GP, kernel ridge regression (Saunders, Gammerman & Vovk 1998), and even kernel PCA (Schölkopf, Smola & Müller 1998) all reduce to *one* linear-algebraic operation — the kernel matrix $K$ — and the difference is only in *which* linear-algebraic problem is solved (QP for SVM, conditional Gaussian for GP, eigenproblem for KPCA).

## Q8. For a graph $G$ with adjacency matrix $A$, what does the second-smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian tell you about connectivity?

**Full answer:**

**Setup (Chung 1997; Godsil & Royle 2001):**
For graph $G$ with $n$ vertices, adjacency $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, degree matrix $D = \text{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_n)$, the **combinatorial Laplacian** is:
$$L = D - A$$
with entries $L_{ii} = d_i$, $L_{ij} = -1$ if $(i,j) \in E$, $L_{ij} = 0$ otherwise.

**Eigenvalues of $L$:** $0 = \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots \le \lambda_n$. The eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 0$ is the constant vector $\mathbf{1}/\sqrt{n}$. This is because $L \mathbf{1} = (D - A)\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{d} - \mathbf{d} = 0$.

**The algebraic connectivity (Fiedler 1973):**
$$\lambda_2 = \min_{\mathbf{x} \perp \mathbf{1}, \mathbf{x} \neq 0} \frac{\mathbf{x}^T L \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{x}} = \min_{\mathbf{x} \perp \mathbf{1}, \mathbf{x} \neq 0} \frac{\sum_{(i,j) \in E} (x_i - x_j)^2}{\sum_i x_i^2}$$

**Fiedler's theorem (Fiedler 1973; Anderson & Morley 1985):**
- $\lambda_2 > 0 \iff G$ is connected.
- $\lambda_2 = 0 \iff G$ has at least two connected components (algebraic multiplicity of $0$ equals number of components).

**More refined:** $\lambda_2$ is the *Cheeger constant* of $G$ up to constants. For any subset $S \subset V$ with $|S| \le n/2$:
$$\frac{\lambda_2}{2} \le \frac{|\partial S|}{\text{vol}(S)} \le \sqrt{2 \lambda_2}$$
where $|\partial S|$ is the number of edges leaving $S$ (Cheeger 1970; Buser 1982; Chung 1997, Theorem 2.2). So large $\lambda_2$ means $G$ is hard to disconnect by removing a few edges (expander graph property).

**Spectral gap** $\lambda_2/\lambda_n$ controls mixing time of random walks on $G$: $\tau_{\text{mix}} = \mathcal{O}(\lambda_n / \lambda_2)$ (Levin, Peres & Wilmer 2017, Chap. 12).

**Worked example:**
- Path $P_n$ (chain of $n$ vertices): $\lambda_k = 2 - 2\cos(k\pi/n)$, $\lambda_2 = 2 - 2\cos(\pi/n) \approx \pi^2/n^2$ (Anderson & Morley 1985).
- Complete $K_n$: $\lambda_2 = n$ — large, consistent with $K_n$ being highly connected.
- Two $K_{n/2}$ disconnected: $\lambda_2 = 0$, multiplicity 2.

**Spectral partitioning (Shi & Malik 2000; Ng, Jordan & Weiss 2002):** The eigenvector $\mathbf{v}_2$ (Fiedler vector) gives a 2-way partition of $G$ that approximately minimizes the normalized cut. This is the bridge from spectral graph theory to graph clustering.

**Deep insight:** The student who memorizes "$\lambda_2 = 0$ means disconnected" misses that $\lambda_2$ is a *quantitative* measure of how connected the graph is, controlling both clustering quality and random walk mixing.

## Q9. Why do we need tensor decompositions when we already have SVD for matrices?

**Full answer:**

**The problem.** Matrices are 2-way (mode-2) arrays. Many real-world data are *higher-order*:

| Data | Tensor shape | Modes |
|---|---|---|
| RGB video | $\mathbb{R}^{T \times H \times W \times 3}$ | time, height, width, channel |
| User–movie–time ratings | $\mathbb{R}^{|U| \times |M| \times |T|}$ | user, movie, time |
| Hyperspectral image | $\mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times B}$ | spatial × spectral |

Reshaping to a matrix and applying SVD *flattens* the multi-way structure — it treats the modes symmetrically but they may have *different* physical meanings and *different* statistics. Tucker 1966 and Kolda & Bader 2009 review.

**CP (CANDECOMP/PARAFAC) decomposition (Carroll & Chang 1970; Harshman 1970):**
$$\mathcal{X} \approx \sum_{r=1}^{R} \lambda_r \, \mathbf{a}_r \circ \mathbf{b}_r \circ \mathbf{c}_r$$
where $\circ$ is outer product. For a 3-way tensor, this is a sum of $R$ rank-1 tensors. Uniqueness holds under mild conditions (Kruskal's condition, Kruskal 1977).

**Tucker decomposition (Tucker 1966):**
$$\mathcal{X} \approx \mathcal{G} \times_1 A \times_2 B \times_3 C$$
where $\mathcal{G}$ is a small "core" tensor and $A, B, C$ are factor matrices along each mode. Tucker is a *multi-way SVD*; CP is Tucker with $\mathcal{G}$ diagonal.

**Why this matters computationally:**
- An $N \times N \times N$ tensor has $N^3$ entries. CP with $R$ components uses only $3NR$ parameters — compression ratio $N^2 / (3R)$.
- SVD on a matrix flattening gives a *rank* in the flattened sense — but the tensor rank (minimum $R$) can be much smaller than any matrix rank (Kolda & Bader 2009, §3).

**Application — recommender systems:**
The Netflix problem: tensor $\mathcal{X}_{u, i, t} = $ rating by user $u$ of item $i$ at time $t$. CP decomposition gives latent factors per mode (user embedding, item embedding, time embedding) — a multi-way extension of matrix factorization used in SVD-based recommenders (Koren, Bell & Volinsky 2009).

**Worked example (Kolda & Bader 2009, §4.1):** For $N = 1000$, CP rank $R = 10$:
- Tensor storage: $10^9$ entries.
- CP parameters: $3 \times 10 \times 10^3 = 30{,}000$.
- Compression ratio: $10^9 / 30{,}000 \approx 33{,}000\times$.

**Deep insight:** The deep student sees tensor decomposition as the *correct* multi-linear generalization of SVD — and recognizes that "reshaping" a tensor into a matrix discards the structure that makes the data multi-modal.

## Q10. Design an end-to-end linear-algebra project for image compression. What matrix factorizations, what error metric, and how do you report results?

**Full answer:**

**Project specification.** Take a grayscale image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ (e.g., $512 \times 512$ Lena, or a hyperspectral scene). Compute SVD, truncate to rank $k$, measure reconstruction error, plot singular value spectrum, comment on what the top singular vectors represent visually.

**Step-by-step (Strang 2020, Lecture 29; Andrews & Patterson 1976):**

1. **Load and center:** Convert to float; subtract row mean and column mean to expose structure.
2. **Compute SVD:** $I = U \Sigma V^T$ using LAPACK `?gesdd` (Anderson et al. 1999) or SciPy `scipy.linalg.svd`. Complexity $\mathcal{O}(\min(m, n) \cdot mn)$.
3. **Plot singular values:** $\sigma_i$ vs. $i$ on log scale. For natural images, expect a power-law decay $\sigma_i \sim i^{-\alpha}$ with $\alpha \approx 0.8$ to $1.2$ (this is the *fractal* property of natural images — Ruderman & Bialek 1994).
4. **Truncate:** $I_k = U_k \Sigma_k V_k^T = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T$.
5. **Measure error:** Multiple metrics (Wang & Bovik 2009):
   - **Frobenius:** $\|I - I_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i>k} \sigma_i^2}$
   - **Spectral:** $\|I - I_k\|_2 = \sigma_{k+1}$
   - **PSNR:** $10 \log_{10}(I_{\max}^2 / \text{MSE})$ dB, with $\text{MSE} = \|I - I_k\|_F^2 / (mn)$.
6. **Report:**
   - Compression ratio: $r = mn / (k(m + n + 1))$.
   - Energy retained: $E_k = \sum_{i \le k} \sigma_i^2 / \sum_i \sigma_i^2$.
   - Plot $E_k$ vs. $k$ (the *explained variance curve*).
7. **Visualize top singular vectors:** $\mathbf{u}_1, \mathbf{v}_1$ as $m \times 1$ and $n \times 1$ images — for natural images, the top left singular vector is approximately uniform (average brightness) and $\mathbf{u}_2, \mathbf{v}_2$ capture horizontal/vertical gradients.
8. **Compare to JPEG:** JPEG uses DCT on $8 \times 8$ blocks (Wallace 1992) — a *local* transform. SVD is *global*. For natural images, SVD at $k = 50$ often beats JPEG at the same compression ratio, especially at low bit-rates (Andrews & Patterson 1976; Strang 2020, Lecture 29).

**Deliverables:**
- Code (Python / MATLAB), with comments.
- The singular value spectrum plot (log scale).
- Original vs. reconstructed images at $k \in \{10, 50, 100, 200\}$.
- PSNR and compression ratio table.
- Discussion: when does SVD beat JPEG? When does it lose?

**Deep insight:** The deep student recognizes that SVD's optimality (Eckart–Young 1936) is *only* in Frobenius/spectral norm — not perceptual (Wang & Bovik 2009). For human-perceived quality, weighted norms or learned metrics (LPIPS, Zhang et al. 2018) matter more.

## DD 1. The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra and Its Four Subspaces
## 基本線性代數定理與其四個子空間

**English:**
The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra (FTLA), as crystallized by Gilbert Strang over four decades of teaching (Strang 1980, 1993, 2005, 2016, 2020), is the *single* most important framework in applied linear algebra. For any matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with rank $r$, the entire theory of $A$ is captured in four subspaces:

$$\underbrace{C(A^T)}_{\text{row space}, \dim=r} \oplus \underbrace{N(A)}_{\text{null space}, \dim=n-r} = \mathbb{R}^n$$
$$\underbrace{C(A)}_{\text{column space}, \dim=r} \oplus \underbrace{N(A^T)}_{\text{left null space}, \dim=m-r} = \mathbb{R}^m$$

The orthogonal complement structure means $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has a solution iff $\mathbf{b} \perp N(A^T)$, and the solution is unique iff $N(A) = \{0\}$. The least-squares solution $A\mathbf{x} \approx \mathbf{b}$ is the projection of $\mathbf{b}$ onto $C(A)$, equivalently the unique vector in $C(A^T)$ minimizing residual. Pseudoinverse $A^+$ computes this in one shot: $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T$.

The dimension formula $\dim N(A) + \dim C(A^T) = n$ is the **rank–nullity theorem** (Sylvester 1851; Frobenius 1878; Strang 1980).

**Numerical example.** Consider $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$. Rows are not multiples of each other (rank 2), so $r = 2$:
- $C(A) \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ — a 2-d plane (the whole $\mathbb{R}^2$).
- $N(A) \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ — 1-d, given by $(x_1, x_2, x_3) = t \cdot (1, -2, 1)$ (verify: $A(1,-2,1)^T = (0, 0)^T$).
- $C(A^T) \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ — 2-d plane orthogonal to $(1, -2, 1)$.
- $N(A^T) \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ — $\{0\}$ since $r = m$.

**中文：**
基本線性代數定理 (FTLA) 由 Gilbert Strang 經過四十年的教學結晶而成 (Strang 1980、1993、2005、2016、2020)，係應用線性代數入面最重要嘅單一框架。對於任何 rank $r$ 嘅矩陣 $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$，$A$ 嘅整個理論由四個子空間完全捕捉：

$$\underbrace{C(A^T)}_{\text{列空間}, \dim=r} \oplus \underbrace{N(A)}_{\text{零空間}, \dim=n-r} = \mathbb{R}^n$$
$$\underbrace{C(A)}_{\text{行空間}, \dim=r} \oplus \underbrace{N(A^T)}_{\text{左零空間}, \dim=m-r} = \mathbb{R}^m$$

正交補嘅結構意味住 $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ 有解若且唯若 $\mathbf{b} \perp N(A^T)$，而解係唯一若且唯若 $N(A) = \{0\}$。最小二乘解 $A\mathbf{x} \approx \mathbf{b}$ 係 $\mathbf{b}$ 投影到 $C(A)$，等價於 $C(A^T)$ 入面使 residual 最小嘅唯一向量。Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse $A^+$ 一步到位計算：$A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T$。

維度公式 $\dim N(A) + \dim C(A^T) = n$ 就係 Sylvester 1851、Frobenius 1878 嘅 **rank–nullity theorem**。

## DD 2. SVD and Low-Rank Approximation — The Eckart–Young Theorem
## SVD 與低秩近似 — Eckart–Young 定理

**English:**
The Singular Value Decomposition $A = U \Sigma V^T$ (Golub & Kahan 1965; Golub & Van Loan 1996, §2.5) is the universal workhorse. Given $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$ (positive singular values), the rank-$k$ truncation
$$A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T$$
is the *unique* minimizer of $\|A - B\|$ over all rank-$k$ matrices $B$, in both Frobenius and spectral norms (Eckart & Young 1936; Mirsky 1960).

**Theorem (Eckart–Young 1936, p. 806):** For any $B$ with $\text{rank}(B) \le k$:
$$\|A - B\|_F \ge \|A - A_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i>k} \sigma_i^2}$$

The proof uses the variational characterization of singular values (Weyl 1912; Horn & Johnson 2012, Theorem 7.3.9). The truncation $A_k$ is *unique* iff $\sigma_k > \sigma_{k+1}$ (Trefethen & Bau 1997, Theorem 4.3).

**Image compression (Andrews & Patterson 1976; Strang 2020, Lecture 29).** A Lena $512 \times 512$ image has $\sigma_i$ decaying roughly as $i^{-1}$ for natural images (Ruderman & Bialek 1994). Storing $A_{50}$ requires only $50 \times (512 + 512 + 1) \approx 51{,}250$ numbers versus $512 \times 512 \approx 262{,}144$ — a $5\times$ compression retaining $\approx 95\%$ energy.

**Numerical example (computed values from Strang 2020, demo):**
| $k$ | Storage | Energy retained | PSNR (dB) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 10,250 | 73% | 22.1 |
| 50 | 51,250 | 95% | 31.5 |
| 100 | 102,500 | 98% | 35.8 |
| 200 | 205,000 | 99.5% | 40.2 |

**Random matrices — universality.** For $A$ with i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}(0, 1/m)$ entries (Marchenko–Pastur 1967 limit), $\sigma_k / \sigma_1$ for $k \approx m \cdot 0.1$ is approximately constant — no compression possible. This is why SVD-compression works on *structured* (natural images, signals) but not on *random* matrices.

**中文：**
奇異值分解 $A = U \Sigma V^T$ (Golub & Kahan 1965；Golub & Van Loan 1996，§2.5) 係通用嘅 workhorse。給定 $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$ (正奇異值)，rank-$k$ 截斷
$$A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T$$
係�所有 rank-$k$ 矩陣 $B$ 入面，唯一令 $\|A - B\|$ 最小嘅一個（無論 Frobenius 定 spectral norm）(Eckart & Young 1936；Mirsky 1960)。

**Eckart–Young 1936 定理**：對任何 rank $\le k$ 嘅 $B$：
$$\|A - B\|_F \ge \|A - A_k\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i>k} \sigma_i^2}$$

證明用到奇異值嘅 variational characterization (Weyl 1912；Horn & Johnson 2012，定理 7.3.9)。截斷 $A_k$ 唯一若且唯若 $\sigma_k > \sigma_{k+1}$ (Trefethen & Bau 1997，定理 4.3)。

**圖像壓縮** (Andrews & Patterson 1976；Strang 2020，第 29 講)：512×512 Lena 圖嘅 $\sigma_i$ 對於自然圖像大概以 $i^{-1}$ 衰減 (Ruderman & Bialek 1994)。儲存 $A_{50}$ 只需要 $50 \times (512 + 512 + 1) \approx 51{,}250$ 個數字，相比 $512 \times 512 \approx 262{,}144$，係 5 倍壓縮而保留約 95% 能量。

## DD 3. Condition Number, Stability, and the Hilbert Matrix Disaster
## 條件數、穩定性與 Hilbert 矩陣災難

**English:**
The condition number $\kappa(A) = \|A\| \cdot \|A^{-1}\| = \sigma_{\max} / \sigma_{\min}$ (Goldberg 1991; Trefethen & Bau 1997, Lecture 12) controls how much relative input error gets amplified:
$$\frac{\|\delta \mathbf{x}\| / \|\mathbf{x}\|}{\|\delta \mathbf{b}\| / \|\mathbf{b}\|} \le \kappa(A)$$

For $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ solved in floating point with $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ (IEEE 754 double, IEEE 1985; Kahan 1996):
$$\text{expected relative error} \approx \kappa(A) \cdot n \cdot \epsilon_{\text{mach}}$$

**Hilbert matrix** $H_n$ (Hilbert 1894) is the canonical ill-conditioned example:
$$(H_n)_{ij} = \frac{1}{i + j - 1}, \quad \kappa_2(H_n) \sim \frac{e^{3.5 n}}{\sqrt{n}}$$
For $n = 10$: $\kappa(H_{10}) \approx 1.6 \times 10^{13}$. For $n = 15$: $\kappa(H_{15}) \approx 2.4 \times 10^{18}$ — beyond double-precision solvability (Trefethen & Bau 1997, p. 121).

**Backward stability** (Wilkinson 1963, 1965; Higham 2002): An algorithm is backward stable if it computes the *exact* solution of a slightly perturbed problem $(A + \delta A)\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{b} + \delta \mathbf{b}$ with $\|\delta A\| / \|A\| \le c \cdot n \cdot \epsilon_{\text{mach}}$.

| Algorithm | Backward stable? | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Householder QR | Yes (to $\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)$) | Householder 1958 |
| LU with partial pivoting | Yes (with $2^{n-1}$ growth factor bound) | Wilkinson 1961 |
| Classical Gram-Schmidt | No (error $\sim \kappa^2$) | Björck 1967 |
| Normal equations ($A^T A \mathbf{x} = A^T \mathbf{b}$) | Loses $\log_{10}(\kappa)$ digits | Trefethen & Bau 1997 |

**Iterative refinement (Jankowski & Woźniakowski 1977; Higham 2002, §12.5):**
1. Solve $A\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = \mathbf{b}$ using any direct solver.
2. Compute residual $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$.
3. Solve $A\delta\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{r}$.
4. Update $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{i+1} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}_i + \delta\mathbf{x}$.
5. Repeat until $\|\mathbf{r}\|$ is small.

Each iteration adds ~$\log_{10}\kappa$ digits, up to machine precision. Cheap (only one more solve) and effective.

**中文：**
條件數 $\kappa(A) = \|A\| \cdot \|A^{-1}\| = \sigma_{\max} / \sigma_{\min}$ (Goldberg 1991；Trefethen & Bau 1997，第 12 講) 控制住相對輸入誤差被放大幾多倍：
$$\frac{\|\delta \mathbf{x}\| / \|\mathbf{x}\|}{\|\delta \mathbf{b}\| / \|\mathbf{b}\|} \le \kappa(A)$$

對於喺 $\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ (IEEE 754 double，IEEE 1985；Kahan 1996) 浮點數下求解 $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$：
$$\text{預期相對誤差} \approx \kappa(A) \cdot n \cdot \epsilon_{\text{mach}}$$

**Hilbert 矩陣** $H_n$ (Hilbert 1894) 係病態嘅典型例子：
$$(H_n)_{ij} = \frac{1}{i + j - 1}, \quad \kappa_2(H_n) \sim \frac{e^{3.5 n}}{\sqrt{n}}$$
$n = 10$：$\kappa(H_{10}) \approx 1.6 \times 10^{13}$。$n = 15$：$\kappa(H_{15}) \approx 2.4 \times 10^{18}$ — 超出 double-precision 可解範圍 (Trefethen & Bau 1997，p. 121)。

**Backward stability** (Wilkinson 1963、1965；Higham 2002)：算法係 backward stable 如果佢計算出輕微擾動問題 $(A + \delta A)\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{b} + \delta \mathbf{b}$ 嘅精確解，而 $\|\delta A\| / \|A\| \le c \cdot n \cdot \epsilon_{\text{mach}}$。

## DD 4. The Least Squares Problem and QR vs. Normal Equations
## 最小二乘問題與 QR vs. 法方程

**English:**
Given overdetermined $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ with $m > n$, no exact solution exists in general. The least-squares solution $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}} \|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2$ solves $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$ (Gauss 1809; Legendre 1805; Stigler 1981 historical note).

**The two paths:**

**Path 1: Normal equations.** Form $A^T A$ ($n \times n$, costs $\frac{1}{2}mn^2$ flops), solve via Cholesky ($\frac{n^3}{6}$ flops). Cheap, but $\kappa(A^T A) = \kappa(A)^2$ — squares the condition number, losing up to $\log_{10}\kappa$ digits (Trefethen & Bau 1997, Lecture 18).

**Path 2: QR factorization.** Compute $A = QR$ with $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, $R \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ upper triangular. Then:
$$\hat{\mathbf{x}} = R^{-1} Q^T \mathbf{b}$$
Costs $\sim 2mn^2$ flops but does *not* square $\kappa$ (Householder 1958; Golub & Van Loan 1996, §5.1).

**Path 3: SVD.** $A = U \Sigma V^T$, $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = V \Sigma^+ U^T \mathbf{b}$. Most expensive ($\sim 4m^2 n$ flops) but most robust — handles rank-deficient $A$ and gives the pseudoinverse solution. The LAPACK routine `?gelsd` (Anderson et al. 1999) uses divide-and-conquer SVD with QR preconditioning.

**Numerical example.** Suppose $A \in \mathbb{R}^{1000 \times 50}$, $\kappa(A) = 10^4$. With normal equations, $\kappa(A^T A) = 10^8$ — lose ~8 digits. With Householder QR, backward stable to $\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{\text{mach}})$. For least squares on a Hilbert-like matrix, SVD is the safest choice.

**Weighted least squares:** $\min \|W(A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b})\|_2$ for diagonal weight $W = \text{diag}(w_1, \dots, w_m)$ reduces to solving $A^T W^T W A \mathbf{x} = A^T W^T W \mathbf{b}$ (Björck 1996, §3).

**Tikhonov regularization (ridge regression):** Solve $\min \|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2$, giving $(A^T A + \lambda I)\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$ (Tikhonov 1943, 1963; Hoerl & Kennard 1970). This *conditioned* matrix has $\kappa \le \kappa(A) + \lambda / \sigma_{\min}(A)$ — better condition. Used throughout statistics (ridge regression, Hoerl & Kennard 1970) and inverse problems (Tikhonov 1963; Engl, Hanke & Neubauer 1996).

**中文：**
對於超定方程 $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ ($m > n$)，一般無精確解。最小二乘解 $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{x}} \|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|_2^2$ 解 $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$ (Gauss 1809；Legendre 1805；Stigler 1981 嘅歷史註腳)。

**兩條路：**

**路徑 1：法方程。** 形成 $A^T A$ ($n \times n$，成本 $\frac{1}{2}mn^2$ flops)，用 Cholesky 求解 ($\frac{n^3}{6}$ flops)。平，但 $\kappa(A^T A) = \kappa(A)^2$ — 條件數平方，最多損失 $\log_{10}\kappa$ 位數 (Trefethen & Bau 1997，第 18 講)。

**路徑 2：QR 分解。** 計算 $A = QR$，$Q \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$，$R \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ 上三角。則：
$$\hat{\mathbf{x}} = R^{-1} Q^T \mathbf{b}$$
成本 $\sim 2mn^2$ flops，但**唔會**將 $\kappa$ 平方 (Householder 1958；Golub & Van Loan 1996，§5.1)。

**路徑 3：SVD。** $A = U \Sigma V^T$，$\hat{\mathbf{x}} = V \Sigma^+ U^T \mathbf{b}$。最貴 ($\sim 4m^2 n$ flops)，但最穩健 — 處理 rank-deficient 嘅 $A$ 並給出 pseudoinverse 解。LAPACK routine `?gelsd` (Anderson et al. 1999) 用 divide-and-conquer SVD 加 QR 預處理。

## DD 5. Eigenvalues, Iterative Methods, and the Power Iteration
## 特徵值、迭代方法與冪迭代

**English:**
Computing eigenvalues $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}$ directly via the characteristic polynomial is *numerically unstable* (Wilkinson 1965, p. 365). Modern algorithms — QR iteration (Francis 1961, 1962), divide-and-conquer (Cuppen 1981), MRRR (Dhillon 1997; Dhillon & Parlett 2004) — use orthogonal similarity transformations to preserve stability.

**Power iteration (von Mises & Pollaczek–Geiringer 1929; Hotelling 1933):** The simplest eigen-algorithm. Initialize $\mathbf{x}_0$ randomly (with component along the top eigenvector), then iterate:
$$\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \frac{A \mathbf{x}_k}{\|A \mathbf{x}_k\|}$$
Converges to $\mathbf{v}_1$ (top eigenvector) at rate $|\lambda_2 / \lambda_1|$ per step (Trefethen & Bau 1997, Lecture 24).
